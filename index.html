Chapter I

Foundations — The Grammar of Chance

Probability begins with a deceptively simple act: listing everything that can happen. The sample space Ω is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment; an event is a subset of Ω. Flip three coins and the sample space is the eight strings HHH, HHT, …, TTT; the event "exactly one head" is the subset {HTT, THT, TTH}. Probability assigns each event a number in [0, 1], and the entire theory — from blackjack to quantum mechanics — is disciplined by three axioms.

ᚨ Definition — Kolmogorov Axioms (1933)

A probability measure P on Ω assigns every event A a number P(A) such that:

  1. P(A) ≥ 0 for all events A (non-negativity);
  2. P(Ω) = 1 (normalization);
  3. For disjoint events A₁, A₂, … (countably many), P(∪ᵢ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ) (countable additivity).

Everything else — conditional probability, expectation, the CLT — is a corollary of these three sentences.

Counting is the engine of classical probability. When every outcome of a finite sample space is equally likely, P(A) = |A| ⁄ |Ω| — so computing a probability is a problem of counting sets. The two fundamental counters are permutations (ordered selections) and combinations (unordered selections):

P(n, r) = n!(n − r)!     C(n, r) = n!r! (n − r)!

With repetition allowed, ordered counting gives nr, and unordered counting gives the stars-and-bars count C(n + r − 1, r). These four quantities generate every poker hand, every lottery edge, and most of discrete probability.

ᛏ Definition — Conditional Probability & Independence

For P(B) > 0, the conditional probability of A given B is

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)P(B)

Events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B) — equivalently P(A | B) = P(A), meaning knowledge of B carries no information about A. Most real-world events are dependent; the Law of Total Probability organizes that dependence: if B₁, …, Bₖ partition Ω, then P(A) = Σᵢ P(A | Bᵢ) P(Bᵢ).

Chapter II

Distributions — The Shapes of Randomness

A random variable X is a function from outcomes to numbers — it turns "the coin came up heads" into X = 1. Its behavior is captured by a probability mass function (PMF) for discrete variables, p(x) = P(X = x), or a probability density function (PDF) for continuous ones, with the cumulative distribution function F(x) = P(X ≤ x) unifying both.

Two numbers summarize any distribution. The expected value E[X] = Σ x p(x) is the long-run average; the variance Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] measures spread. Higher moments refin the picture: skewness γ₁ = E[(X−μ)³] ⁄ σ³ detects asymmetry, and kurtosis (excess γ₂ = E[(X−μ)⁴] ⁄ σ⁴ − 3) measures tail weight.

ᛒ Definitions — The Great Four
  • Binomial(n, p): the number of successes in n independent trials, each succeeding with probability p. P(X = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ (1−p)n−k; μ = np, σ² = np(1−p). Models: streaks, win counts.
  • Poisson(λ): the limit of Binomial as n → ∞, p → 0 with np = λ fixed — counting rare events. P(X = k) = e−λλᵏ ⁄ k!; μ = σ² = λ.
  • Geometric(p): the waiting time until the first success. P(X = k) = (1−p)k−1p; μ = 1/p. It is memoryless: P(X > m+n | X > m) = P(X > n).
  • Normal(μ, σ²): the continuous limit of aggregated noise. f(x) = 1σ√(2π) exp(−(x−μ)² ⁄ 2σ²). Symmetric; skew 0, excess kurtosis 0. Its CDF Φ is computed here via an erf rational approximation, and its quantile Φ⁻¹ by bisection on Φ.

Chapter III

Core Theorems — Why Order Emerges from Chaos

Three theorems elevate probability from bookkeeping to law. The Law of Large Numbers says averages converge to expectations; the Central Limit Theorem says the shape of that convergence is always the same bell; and Bayes' Theorem tells you exactly how evidence should move belief.

ᛇ Theorem — Law of Large Numbers

Let X₁, X₂, … be i.i.d. with E[Xᵢ] = μ, and let p̂n = (X₁ + … + Xₙ) ⁄ n. The weak law: for any ε > 0, P(|p̂n − μ| > ε) → 0 as n → ∞. The strong law (Kolmogorov): P(p̂n → μ) = 1 — convergence happens on almost every infinite sequence. Chebyshev's inequality makes the rate explicit: P(|p̂n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ² ⁄ (n ε²) — the ±3σₜ band drawn in the simulator below shrinks like 1 ⁄ √n.

ᛚ Theorem — Central Limit Theorem

For i.i.d. Xᵢ with mean μ and finite variance σ², the standardized sample mean satisfies

p̂n − μσ ⁄ √n ⟶ N(0, 1)   in distribution as n → ∞.

The miracle is universality: dice, coins, incomes, lifetimes — any population with finite variance yields bell-shaped sample means. The standard error σ ⁄ √n quantifies exactly how sampling size purchases precision: quadrupling n halves the spread.

ᛞ Theorem — Bayes' Theorem

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | ¬A)P(¬A)

Posterior belief = prior belief × likelihood of the evidence, renormalized. The classic trap — the base-rate fallacy — is reading P(disease | positive test) as if it were the sensitivity P(positive | disease). When disease prevalence is 1%, even a 99%-sensitive, 95%-specific test yields a posterior of only ≈ 17%. The denominator carries the base rate; intuition never does.

Chapter IV

The Gambler's Path — A Cautionary Calculus

Casinos do not gamble; they engineer expected value. The expected value of a bet that pays b units on a win (probability p) and loses the 1-unit stake otherwise is EV = p·b − (1 − p). European roulette's single number pays 35:1 against success probability 1 ⁄ 37, giving EV = −1 ⁄ 37 ≈ −2.70% per unit staked; the American double-zero wheel costs −2 ⁄ 38 ≈ −5.26%. The house edge is the negative of your EV — and it is assessed on every unit wagered, every spin.

ᛟ Theorem — Gambler's Ruin

A gambler starts with i units, wins each round with probability p (q = 1 − p), gains or loses 1 unit per round, and stops on reaching 0 (ruin) or N (target). Solving the recurrence u(i) = p·u(i+1) + q·u(i−1) with boundary conditions u(0) = 0, u(N) = 1:

P(reach N before ruin) = 1 − (q ⁄ p)i1 − (q ⁄ p)N   for p ≠ ½,    and   i ⁄ N   for p = ½.

When p < ½ the formula is brutal: raise the target N and survival probability collapses geometrically. Persistence is not a strategy; it is a denominator.

ᛜ Analysis — The Martingale Illusion

Double your bet after every loss; on the first win you recoup everything plus one base unit. The scheme converts many small wins into rare, ruinous losses — the probability of busting after k consecutive losses is qk, never zero. Crucially, expectation is linear and indifferent to staking schemes: every spin still carries EV = (p − q)·bet < 0, so no sequence of negative-EV bets can sum to a positive-EV system. A finite bankroll merely decides when the arithmetic collects.

ᛇ Fallacies — The Gambler's & the Hot Hand

Independent trials have no memory: after five heads, P(heads) is still p — "tails is due" is the gambler's fallacy. Its mirror, the hot-hand fallacy, projects streaks onto independent processes and expects them to continue. Both are refuted by the same line of algebra: P(A | B) = P(A) when A and B are independent. Streaks are not information; they are realizations.

Chapter V

Advanced Methods — Measuring the Unseen

Mature statistics is a technology for extracting signal from randomness. Monte Carlo methods replace hard integrals with cheap samples; hypothesis testing formalizes "is this real?"; Bayesian updating turns evidence into movement of belief; and Shannon entropy prices uncertainty itself in bits.

ᚨ Method — Monte Carlo Estimation

To estimate π, throw uniform darts at the unit square: the fraction landing inside the quarter circle is π ⁄ 4, so π̂ = 4·(# inside) ⁄ N. By the CLT the error is O(1 ⁄ √N) — quadrupling your darts halves the error. The same σ ⁄ √N law governs every simulation on this page: scatter, then settle.

ᛈ Method — Hypothesis Testing

Testing H₀: p = p₀ for a coin with K heads in n flips uses the z-statistic

z = K − n p₀√(n p₀ (1 − p₀))

with two-sided p-value 2(1 − Φ(|z|)). Rejecting a true H₀ is a Type I error (controlled at α, here 0.05); failing to reject a false H₀ is a Type II error. Power — the chance of catching a real deviation — grows with sample size; significance is a property of the evidence, not a switch.

ᛊ Method — Sequential Bayesian Updating

Competing hypotheses (fair coin θ₁ = 0.5 vs. biased θ₂ = 0.7) update by multiplying the prior odds by the likelihood ratio: heads multiplies P(H | θ₁) ⁄ P(H | θ₂), tails the converse. In log-odds form each flip adds a fixed increment — evidence accumulates linearly in the log, so posterior probability sigmoids toward the truth. Bayes factors make belief revision arithmetic rather than opinion.

ᛞ Definition — Shannon Entropy

H(X) = −Σx p(x) log₂ p(x) — the average surprise, in bits, of sampling X. For k outcomes, H is maximized by the uniform distribution at H = log₂ k bits, and collapses to 0 when a single outcome is certain. Entropy and variance are twin measures of scatter: variance weighs squared distance, entropy counts unexpected symbols. A fair die carries log₂ 6 ≈ 2.585 bits; a loaded die squanders them. The gate opens at maximum entropy; knowledge closes it.
